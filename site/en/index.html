Answering the finite-outcome question of Aloy et al. (2024), arXiv:2312.09278v2, end of §IV.B.3, p. 17: an extension of their binary spin-one theorem

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Imagine a device with a dial: turning it changes the chances of its possible results. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang ask whether every probability pattern obeying a particular restriction on that variation can come from quantum mechanics, and answer yes for any finite number of results.

Each probability curve may combine a constant with sine and cosine waves repeating once or twice per full turn. These are called trigonometric polynomials of degree at most two; here they define general ‘spin-one’ patterns. Every curve must stay nonnegative, and all curves must add to one at every angle. Aloy, Galley, Jones, Ludescher and Müller proved quantum realizability for two results in work first posted in 2023 and published in 2024. Their question about three or more results is what this manuscript answers.

For a quantum realization, picture three arrows whose lengths stay fixed while their directions turn backward, remain still, and turn forward with the dial. These represent complex amplitudes: their squared lengths add to one, forming a pure state of a three-level quantum system, or qutrit. A measurement uses fixed matrices—square arrays of numbers—to assign probabilities. The matrices must be positive, meaning their associated quadratic expressions are nonnegative for every input, and sum to the identity, which leaves vectors unchanged. This measurement is called a POVM. One state and measurement must work at all angles. Differences between turning rates produce only the allowed wave frequencies. Pure qutrit realizations for already-quantum families were known; the new task is showing every allowed general family is quantum.

The construction first represents each probability separately by a positive three-by-three matrix, called a Gram matrix, whose quadratic expression on the three rotating arrows gives that curve. Aloy and colleagues’ scalar factorization result already supported this step. The authors also prove it directly: subtract the curve’s minimum and shift its position to angle zero. The remainder factors into one minus cosine times a nonnegative constant-plus-first-wave expression. They translate those factors into a positive matrix, then restore the minimum and angle shift. The problem is that these separate matrices need not sum to something usable as one normalized measurement.

The key move averages each matrix with the matrix obtained by reversing coordinate order and swapping rows with columns. The authors show that positivity and the entire probability curve survive. Afterwards, the two neighboring off-diagonal entries agree, as do the two outer diagonal entries. Summing over outcomes gives the constant curve one. Its twice-per-turn coefficient must vanish; its once-per-turn coefficient, the sum of two now-equal entries, must also vanish. Consequently the total matrix is diagonal. This is an algebraic argument: the arrow analogy helps explain the objects but does not prove the result.

The three diagonal numbers are nonnegative and sum to one. Their square roots become the state’s arrow lengths, with equal outer lengths. Rescaling each outcome matrix’s rows and columns by the reciprocal square roots produces measurement matrices summing to the identity. If a diagonal number is zero, positivity forces every corresponding outcome row and column to vanish. The authors normalize only occupied coordinates and fill unused coordinates with equal shares of the identity, which cannot affect this state’s probabilities. Thus exact boundary cases work too. The state and measurement may depend on the probability family.

Three levels sometimes really are needed. For example, at dial angle θ, let one outcome’s probability be p₊(θ) = 1/2 + (cos θ + cos 2θ)/8, and the other be its complement. Both stay between one quarter and three quarters. This contains both frequencies, whereas two levels with the permitted rotation rates supply at most one positive frequency difference. Nor can one fixed state serve every family by changing measurements: a probability (1 + cos 2θ)/2 forces the middle-rate component to disappear, preventing a cos θ pattern. A four-coordinate counterexample also shows reversal averaging can leave nonzero off-diagonal entries whose wave contributions cancel. This normalization method therefore does not automatically extend to higher dimensions.

The universal conclusions have written proofs. The manuscript reports a Lean declaration covering equality for all finite outcome sets with at least three elements; its stated conclusion does not include the stronger pure-state, equal-outer-length form or the additional consequences. Reported finite and symbolic checks support selected identities and examples, not universal coverage. Manos, Weilenmann and Navascués had already characterized related quantum models by positive matrices with diagonal sums; this construction supplies that condition automatically here. The advance is simultaneous quantum realization of every allowed finite-outcome spin-one family, not an identification of whole probabilistic theories. Known higher-spin differences remain unchanged, and infinitely many outcomes are outside the result.
